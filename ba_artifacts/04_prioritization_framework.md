# Improvement Prioritization Framework

The dissertation identifies four recurring improvement areas but does not provide a measured cost, benefit, or intervention outcome for each. The prioritization below is therefore a **proposed implementation framework**, not a claim that one theme occurred more frequently than another.

## Prioritization criteria

Four criteria are used to move from findings to a practical sequence of action:

1. **Evidence breadth**: whether the issue appears across the dissertation's theme analysis, qualitative review, and strength/weakness mapping.
2. **Student-experience relevance**: how directly the issue affects the learning experience described in the feedback.
3. **Change leverage**: whether one intervention could improve multiple related complaints.
4. **Implementation complexity**: the likely coordination required before change can be tested.

No numeric score is assigned because the dissertation does not provide validated cost, effort, or theme-frequency values that would support a defensible weighted calculation.

## Proposed priority sequence

| Priority | Improvement area | Why it is prioritized | Main dependency | Initial action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Course structure and pacing | Recurring concerns around organization, pacing, time allocation, and course delivery can be addressed through a standardized design approach that affects multiple modules. | Programme-level agreement on minimum course-design standards | Define a standard syllabus structure with weekly objectives, time allocation, and review points. |
| Tier 1 | Teaching interaction and responsiveness | Positive and negative feedback both point to instructor interaction as a strong driver of experience. Improvement can be targeted through faculty development without redesigning an entire curriculum. | Faculty engagement and ownership | Pilot interactive-teaching guidance and structured participation methods in selected modules. |
| Tier 2 | Practical learning relevance | Feedback identifies the value of applied learning and frustration where labs or content feel disconnected from practice. The potential impact is high, but curriculum redesign may require more effort and coordination. | Curriculum ownership, teaching resources, assessment alignment | Review high-concern modules for opportunities to add applied cases, projects, or clearer lab interpretation. |
| Tier 2 | Structured student interaction and assessment support | Students value discussion and feedback opportunities, while some concerns involve weak interaction and assessment organization. Improvement may span teaching, assessment, and support processes. | Assessment design and timetable capacity | Introduce structured Q&A or mid-semester feedback checkpoints and review assessment communication. |

## Why Tier 1 does not mean 'most common'

The source analysis confirms that all four areas are material. Tier 1 indicates that these areas appear suitable for earlier intervention because they combine broad relevance with relatively clear actions. It does **not** mean the dissertation proved they had the highest complaint count or return on investment.

## Decision rule for a live implementation

If this process were implemented with operational data, the qualitative sequence above should be replaced or supplemented with a measured scorecard using:

- percentage of analyzable comments linked to the issue;
- number of modules affected;
- persistence across reporting periods;
- severity or escalation rate;
- estimated implementation effort;
- expected reach of the intervention;
- post-intervention change in the target theme.

That would allow prioritization to shift from evidence-informed judgement to a repeatable portfolio of improvement initiatives.
